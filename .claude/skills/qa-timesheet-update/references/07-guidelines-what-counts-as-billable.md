# Guidelines — What counts as billable

**Applies to:** Everyone who logs time.

**The rule in one sentence:** an hour is **billable** when you can point at the client project it helped deliver. Everything else is **non-billable** — including work that is genuinely valuable and makes the company better.

---

## Two kinds of billable hour

You choose between these on the log form. Both are charged to the client. The split exists so the company can tell the difference between a month spent building and a month spent in meetings.

**Delivery** — building, testing, designing and documenting. The work the client is actually paying for.
Attach the ticket if there is one. If there isn't, write in the notes what the work was. A chargeable hour has to point at something real.

**Meeting** — any call, discussion, workshop, training session or walkthrough about a client's project, whether or not the client is in the room.
No ticket needed. Always say what it was about, and if it ran over half an hour, say why you had to stay for the whole thing.

---

## Dos and don'ts

The worked examples further down tell you whether an hour **is** billable. This section is about whether it should have happened the way it did.

**Do** log a meeting against the project it serves.
**Don't** bill a meeting just because you were in the room. If none of it was about your work, it isn't your billable hour.

**Do** say what the meeting was about, every time. Past half an hour, say why you needed to stay for all of it.
**Don't** leave your lead to guess from a project name and a duration.

**Do** name who else was there. A meeting has no ticket behind it, so the project and the people are the only proof it happened.
**Don't** log a room full of people as an hour that nobody else's timesheet backs up.

**Do** keep every meeting short and focused. Take the shortest one that answers the question, and leave once your part is done.
**Don't** treat your meeting allowance as time to spend. It's the most you may bill, not an amount you owe. Fifteen minutes that settles the question beats an hour that fills the slot.

**Do** flag a client meeting that keeps dragging or overrunning — tick Blocked on the entry, or raise it in your week wrap-up. Overlong meetings are a delivery risk your lead can actually do something about.
**Don't** just absorb the time quietly. A meeting that eats the afternoon costs the project either way, and if nobody flags it, nobody can fix it.

**Do** only join the meetings your role needs. UAT prep, demos and client workshops belong to the roles running them.
**Don't** sit in just to look involved. Every meeting counts against your own role's daily allowance, and your lead is told when you go over.

**Do** log a production incident during working hours as billable time on that project.
**Don't** log out-of-hours firefighting as a time entry. That goes on the week wrap-up as night work, which never fills the 40-hour week. A deployment earns a hardship allowance, and on-call support is the CTO's call.

**Do** declare real evening or weekend work you did on your own initiative as extra work on the week tab — what you did, why it had to be out of hours, and a ticket or link if there is one.
**Don't** log it as a time entry, and don't round it up. It never counts towards your week or your billable hours. Your lead acknowledges or disputes every declaration, and a disputed one stays on your record.

**Do** keep delivery the bigger half of your week.
**Don't** let meetings crowd it out. Every role has a meeting allowance — an hour a day for engineers, 1.5h a day for a Senior QA automation engineer, more for leads — and your lead is told when you go over it.

## Worked examples

The reason in the last column matters more than the verdict, because that's what you apply to a case that isn't listed here.

One important caveat: these say whether an hour is billable, **not whose hour it should be**. That depends on your role. A UAT walkthrough or a client workshop is usually a project lead's or a business analyst's to run, and an engineer with a whole week of them has a staffing problem, not a billing one.

| Activity | Billable? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Meeting the BA or project lead to clarify requirements for a client's project | Billable | You can't deliver the project without it |
| An internal discussion about a technical problem on a client's project | Billable | It's work on that project, whether or not the client is in the room |
| Running a UAT walkthrough with the client | Billable | Part of delivering the project and getting it accepted |
| Preparing for a UAT session or a demo | Billable | Project delivery work |
| Attending a general engineering or team meeting | Non-billable | Company-internal — no single client can be charged for it |
| Receiving training, or being mentored | Non-billable | It builds the company's skills, not a client's business |
| Mentoring somebody generally | Non-billable | Company work — it's what a senior's non-billable hours are for |
| Internal AI research, tooling and knowledge sharing | Non-billable | Company capability. It shows up in your rating, not on an invoice |
| A government or industry workshop for a client's project | Billable | Project work, wherever it happens — bill it to that project |
| A government or industry invitation with no project behind it — an open house, a networking night | Non-billable | Company representation. Real and expected, but not one client's to pay for |
| Fixing a production defect on a client's system | Billable | Their system, their project — log it as an incident as well |
| Building or maintaining an internal tool | Non-billable | Nobody is being invoiced for it |
| Writing a client's handover or user documentation | Billable | Part of what they're paying for. If no ticket covers it, say so in the notes |
| Running a training session for a client | Billable | You're delivering their project — log it as a meeting, which needs no ticket |

## The non-billable categories

When work is non-billable you pick a category instead of a project. Here's what each one covers. All of it is real work; none of it can be charged to a client.

| Category | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Non-project meeting | Any meeting with no client project behind it — team, department, all-hands |
| Team standup | Your own team's standup. A client project's standup belongs to that project |
| Company training | Training you receive, and training the company runs for itself. Training you deliver to a client is that client's project |
| Company documentation | The company's own documentation — process, standards, runbooks. A client's documentation is part of their deliverable |
| Internal tools | Building or maintaining anything nobody is invoiced for |
| Process improvement | Making the way the company works better |
| Company initiative | Company-wide programmes and events |
| Competition | Internal competitions and hackathons |
| AI knowledge sharing | Internal AI research and tooling, and sharing what you found |
| Buddy mentoring | Mentoring, or being mentored, one to one |
| Other | Anything internal that fits nowhere above — say what it was in the notes |

## What this means for your work

This section is grouped by the kind of work you do, not your job title. It answers the part the examples above leave out: which billable activities are normally yours to do.

The page has sections for Engineering, Quality assurance, Analysis, Design, Content and Leads. Two were expanded in the screenshot:

### Quality assurance

- **Delivery** — test planning, writing and running test cases, defect triage and regression runs against a client's system. For automation, the suite, the page objects and the fixtures written for that client's application are part of that client's deliverable.
- **Billable meetings** — defect triage calls, requirement walkthroughs, UAT support, and release go/no-go for that project.
- **Not billable** — the automation framework itself, CI plumbing, tool evaluations, and anything reusable across clients. Same skill, same day, but a different question: could you invoice one client for it?

### Leads

- **Delivery** — leading a client's delivery is billable to that project: solution design, unblocking people, client escalations, reviewing that project's work.
- **Billable meetings** — client status calls, steering meetings and escalations for that project.
- **Not billable** — people management, timesheet review, one-to-ones, counselling, hiring and appraisals. That's what a lead's fourteen non-billable hours a week are for, and the benchmark already assumes them.

## Your working hours

Your 8 working hours run from when you start. Start at 8am and your day ends at 5pm. The timesheet records what you did inside those hours, and that's the capacity every report measures you against.

**After your own end of day, the time is yours.** Carrying on because you want to is welcome, but **don't log it**. Evening work isn't billable and it doesn't top up your week. The point is to get the work done inside office hours, when the people who can unblock you are around — not to save it for home.

**If you want that work seen, declare it as extra work on the week tab.** Say what you did, why it had to be outside working hours, and how long it took. It stays outside the week and is never billable. Your lead acknowledges or disputes each declaration, and it appears on your monthly review.

**Don't declare time you spent teaching yourself.** AI is changing how this work gets done, so evenings and weekends spent learning are well spent — juniors especially, though it holds for anyone. Keep it off the extra work card. Learning isn't delivery, and there's no honest way to measure it.

**Three other records already exist for the edge cases:**

- An out-of-hours deployment or call-out is **night work**, recorded on the week wrap-up.
- A day you're away is **leave**, filed with Mark leave on My timesheet. It takes those hours off your capacity, so never log around it.
- A production incident **inside** working hours is just an ordinary billable entry.

## Your meeting allowance

Your role gives you a daily allowance of billable meeting time. A **Senior QA automation engineer gets 1.5h a day** (read live from /guidelines on 18 Aug 2026). The general ladder is an hour a day for engineers, and more for seniors and leads. Check `/guidelines` for your own role rather than assuming.

It's counted across the month and scaled to the days you actually worked, so one workshop-heavy day never breaches it on its own. Go over the monthly total and your lead is told.

It's a ceiling, not a quota. Nobody is short of anything for using less of it.

---

## Night work and hardship allowance

- **A production deployment outside working hours earns a hardship allowance** if it ran an hour or more. What it pays depends on how long it ran, and those bands are in the employee handbook, not in this tool.
- **On-call and production support outside working hours are the CTO's decision.** They might earn the allowance and they might not. Record it either way. An on-call rotation is being planned, but until it exists there's no roster to point at, so each case is decided individually.
- **Working late on your own work isn't hardship.** That's extra work — declare it on the week tab if you want it on the record. It earns no allowance and no late start. What it earns is being seen.
- **Whichever it was, the hours never count towards the 40-hour week.** Putting a 1am call-out inside the week would make your capacity look bigger than it was, and would quietly reward being paged.
- **Coming in later the next working day needs the CTO's approval.** Agree it first, then mark it on the entry — 1, 2, 3 hours or half a day. Marking it takes those hours off that day straight away, so you're never measured against a morning you were told to take. The CTO approves or rejects it afterwards. A rejected one goes back to expecting a full day, and your declaration stays on the record — which is exactly why you agree it first.
- **Record every call-out, whether or not you started late.** Only you know what the night actually cost you, which is why the late start is something you declare rather than something the system works out for you.

## Billable is not the same as delivered

A meeting about a client's project is billable, but that isn't permission to be in one.

Your role's billable target is aimed at the work the role exists to do — an engineer is hired to build, a QA to test, an analyst to specify. A week that hits its billable target entirely through meetings has hit a number, not a target.

So the two are counted separately. Every week and every month reports how much of your billable time was meetings: on your own timesheet, on your lead's review of it, and in the Monday summary they receive. Past your role's allowance you're flagged, and the flag says by how much.

---

## How a billable meeting is checked

A meeting has no ticket, so the record has to carry the proof instead.

Every billable meeting says what it was about and names who was there, and your lead can see whether the people you named logged that hour too. Timesheets that agree are the evidence. One that stands alone invites a question.

The hours are watched as well. Your role's meeting allowance is counted across the month, and past it your lead sees a **Meeting-heavy** flag. A lead who doubts an hour can query it, cut it, or move it to non-billable — so an hour that didn't happen the way it was logged is never a safe thing to write down.

---

## If you get it wrong

Your lead can reclassify an entry when they review the week, and you'll see that they did it and why.

It's a correction, not an accusation. The borderline cases on this page are borderline for everybody.
